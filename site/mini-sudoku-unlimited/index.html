How to play Mini Sudoku

  1. Pick a square. Tap any empty square – its row, column and box light up so you can see what it is up against. Grey squares with dark numbers are given and cannot be changed.
  2. Place a number. Tap a number on the pad below the board (or type it). Each row, each column and each of the six 2×3 boxes must end up with 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 exactly once.
  3. Use notes when unsure. Switch on Notes to write small candidates in a square. They are cleared automatically when the number is placed elsewhere in the same row, column or box.
  4. Watch for red. A number that repeats in a row, column or box turns red at once, so you never get far down a wrong path without noticing.
  5. Finish the grid. The puzzle is solved the moment the last square is filled correctly. Share your time, or go straight to the next board.

Why practise with unlimited mini sudoku?

A 6×6 sudoku looks small, but it uses exactly the same thinking as the full 9×9 puzzle: scanning a box for the one place a number fits, and spotting a square whose row and column already rule out every number but one. Because the board is small, you can do a full puzzle in a minute or two – perfect for a coffee break – and you get many more repetitions of those two skills than with a big sudoku. One puzzle a day is a nice habit; a dozen in a row is how you actually get fast. When 6×6 starts to feel easy, the Expert level gives you a classic 9×9 board with the same tools.

Mini sudoku strategy: five techniques that solve every board here

1. Scan one number across the boxes

Pick a number – say 3 – and look at every box that does not have it yet. Each 3 already on the board blocks its whole row and column. Often only one square in a box is left unblocked, and that is where the 3 goes. Sweep the numbers 1 to 6 one after another; this alone solves most Easy boards.

2. Look for the last number in a line

A row, column or box with five numbers filled is a free square: the missing number is the only one left. On a 6×6 board this happens far more often than on a 9×9 board, so check for it after every move.

3. Count what a square can see

For a single empty square, list the numbers already in its row, its column and its box. If five different numbers appear, the sixth is the answer. This "only candidate" step is what our hint calls "its row, column and box already hold every other number".

4. Use the box pairs

The six boxes sit in three bands of two. If a number appears in one box of a band, it is ruled out of that row in the neighbouring box. On a 6×6 board each box only has two rows, so knowing one row is ruled out often pins the number to the other row straight away.

5. Write notes only where it pays

On Hard boards, pencil in the two or three candidates of the most crowded squares rather than every square. As soon as one of them is filled, the notes in the same row, column and box are cleaned up for you and a new single usually appears.

Common mini sudoku mistakes

  • Forgetting the box. A number can be fine in its row and column and still clash inside its 2×3 box. Remember the boxes are wide (three columns) and short (two rows).
  • Guessing on Hard. No board here needs a guess. If you are stuck, go back to scanning each number across the boxes – there is always one forced square somewhere.
  • Filling notes everywhere. Pencil marks on every square slow you down on a small board. Use them on the two or three squares that matter.
  • Racing too early. Speed comes from spotting the two techniques above at a glance. Solve Easy and Medium cleanly first, then chase times on the daily.

Worked example: one box, one number

Imagine the top-left box (rows 1–2, columns 1–3) still needs a 5. Row 1 already has a 5 further to the right, so neither square of row 1 in this box can take it. That leaves row 2 – but column 1 also has a 5 lower down the board, and column 2 is already filled. Only row 2, column 3 is left, so the 5 goes there. Two given numbers outside the box were enough to place a number inside it, without any guessing – and that new 5 now blocks row 2 and column 3 for the rest of the board.

What are the rules of mini sudoku?

The board is 6 squares wide and 6 tall, split into six boxes of 2 rows × 3 columns. Place the numbers 1 to 6 so that no number repeats in any row, any column or any box. Some numbers are given at the start and cannot be changed.

Does every board have exactly one answer?

Yes. Before a board is shown, our solver fills it using only logical steps – no guessing. If it cannot finish, the board is thrown away. A board that can be completed step by step this way can only have one solution.

What is the difference between the levels?

Easy, Medium and Hard are 6×6 boards. Easy starts with 18 numbers, Medium with 13, and Hard with as few as our generator can leave while keeping one logical answer – usually 9 to 11. Expert is a classic 9×9 sudoku with 3×3 boxes.

How do notes (pencil marks) work?

Press Notes to switch note mode on. Now a number you tap is written small in the corner of the square instead of being placed. Tap it again to remove it. When you place a real number, the same note is cleared from its row, column and box automatically.

What does the Hint button do?

If a number you placed is wrong, Hint removes it and tells you which square it was. Otherwise it fills one square that can be worked out right now and explains why – for example that a 4 can only go in one square of a row, or that a square has only one number left.

Does the daily Mini Sudoku get harder during the week?

Yes. Our daily board follows a weekly ramp: Monday starts with 18 given numbers, and each day gives fewer, down to Saturday's 11 and a Sunday board with only the minimum our generator can leave. Every day still has exactly one answer that can be found without guessing.

Is my progress saved if I close the page?

Yes. The board you are working on, your notes, hints and the clock are saved in your own browser after every move. Open the same puzzle again and you continue where you stopped. Nothing is sent to our servers.

Can I play with a keyboard?

Yes. Click a square, then use the arrow keys to move, type 1–6 (or 1–9 on Expert) to place a number, Backspace to clear it and N to switch notes on or off.

Undo never affects anything. Hints are shown in the result you share, but your solved count, best times and daily streak still go up.

How is Mini Sudoku different from Queens, Tango and Zip?

Mini Sudoku is about numbers: each 1 to 6 once per row, column and box. Queens is about placing one queen per row, column and colour region, Tango balances suns and moons, and Zip draws one path through every square. All of them are free and unlimited here.
