How to play Zip

  1. Start on 1. Press the square with the number 1 and drag your finger or mouse to draw the path. You can also tap neighbouring squares one at a time.
  2. Visit the numbers in order. Your line must reach 2 before 3, 3 before 4 and so on. The game will not let you enter a number out of turn.
  3. Fill every square. The path has to pass through every square on the board exactly once. It moves only up, down, left or right – never diagonally – and it can never cross itself.
  4. Respect the walls. A thick bar between two squares is a wall; your path must go around it.
  5. Finish on the highest number. When the line covers the whole grid and ends on the last number, the puzzle is solved.

Made a wrong turn? Drag back along your line to erase it, or tap any square of the path to continue from there. Stuck on a board from somewhere else – perhaps today's official one? Enter it into the Zip solver and reveal the path a few squares at a time.

Zip strategy: patterns that solve most boards

1. Corners have only two doors

A corner square touches just two neighbours. Unless the corner holds 1 or the final number, the path must come in through one of them and leave through the other – so both of those links are fixed before you draw anything. Mark the corners first and the edges of the board often fall into place.

2. Squares with two exits are fixed

The same idea works anywhere: if a square has only two open neighbours left – because of walls, the edge of the board or your own line – the path must use both. Walls create these squares all the time, which is why boards with walls are often easier to read than they look.

3. Never leave a dead end behind

Before each move, check the squares next to your line. If one of them now has only a single open neighbour, the path can enter it but never leave, so it must be the very last square. If it is not the final number, you have taken a wrong turn – back up straight away.

4. Do not cut the board in two

If your line separates a group of empty squares from the rest of the board, you can never come back to finish them. Sweeping along the edge, or filling one region completely before leaving it, keeps every empty square reachable.

5. Count the distance to the next number

The squares between two numbers must be filled before you reach the second one. If 4 and 5 are right next to each other but a whole corner of the board is still empty, that corner has to be visited later – on the way from 5 to 6 or further on.

6. Think in chessboard colours

Colour the grid like a chessboard. Every step changes colour, so on a board with an odd number of squares the path must start and end on the more common colour. This quick check tells you which of two routes can possibly be right without drawing either of them.

Common Zip mistakes

  • Racing to the next number. The shortest way from 3 to 4 is rarely the right one; the empty squares around it still need to be filled later.
  • Ignoring walls. A wall is not just an obstacle – it reduces the exits of the squares beside it, and those squares are often forced.
  • Leaving single gaps. One empty square trapped between your line and the edge is almost always a dead end.
  • Starting over too quickly. Drag back to the point where things went wrong instead of restarting; most of your path is usually right.

Worked example: a corner that solves itself

Picture the top-left corner of a board where 1 sits two squares to the right of the corner and no number is in the corner itself. The corner square can only be reached from the square below it or the square to its right, so the path must use both links: below-corner → corner → right-of-corner. That right-hand neighbour sits directly next to 1. Unless the path is to end in this corner, the line from 1 has to start by turning left into it, run through the corner and continue straight down the left edge. Three squares are fixed before you draw a single line – and the next corner usually works the same way.

No. Unlimited Dailies is an independent site and is not connected to LinkedIn. We only use the general rules of the puzzle style; every board here is generated by our own code and looks different from the official game.

Does every Zip board have exactly one answer?

Yes. Before a board is shown, our solver searches for a second path. If one exists, the generator adds a number or a wall and checks again, so every board has exactly one solution.

Do I have to fill every square?

Yes. The path must cover every square of the grid exactly once and finish on the highest number. Reaching the last number early is not allowed, which is why the game stops you if you try.

What are the thick lines between some squares?

They are walls. Your path cannot cross a wall, so treat it like the edge of the board. Walls appear on Hard, Expert and Daily boards and often force the path into a single direction.

How do I go back if I took a wrong turn?

Drag back over your own line to erase it square by square, tap any square of your path to continue from there, or press Undo to remove your last stroke. Restart clears the board.

What does the Hint button do?

If your path has gone the wrong way, Hint cuts it back to the last correct square. If everything is right so far, it draws the next few squares of the answer, up to the next number, and highlights where it stopped.

Can I play Zip with a keyboard?

Yes. Click the board and press any arrow key to start on 1, then use the arrow keys to extend the path. Moving back onto the previous square erases it, and Backspace removes the last square.

When does the daily Zip change?

The daily board changes at midnight UTC and is the same for everyone that day. Solving it on the day adds to your streak, and older dailies stay playable in the archive below.

Is there a Zip solver?

Yes. If you are stuck on any Zip board, open our Zip solver, tap in the numbers and walls from your board and reveal the path five squares at a time, or all at once.
